# Missing Economic Data: Impact on the Economic Evaluation of AI-Assisted Diabetic Retinopathy Screening

## 1. Purpose

This analysis examines how missing, incomplete or uncertain economic information affects the evaluation of AI-assisted diabetic retinopathy screening in the NHS.

The purpose is to identify which missing inputs could materially change an economic conclusion, explain the direction of possible bias and establish how these limitations should be handled in the research.

The absence of a reported cost or outcome must not be interpreted as evidence that the cost or outcome is zero.

## 2. Why missing economic information matters

Economic evaluations compare the costs and consequences of alternative healthcare pathways.

An evaluation can be misleading if it includes the purchase price of an AI system but excludes the wider resources needed to implement, operate and monitor it.

Similarly, an evaluation may overstate the benefits of AI if it assumes that improved diagnostic performance automatically leads to improved patient outcomes or reduced healthcare expenditure.

The systematic review by El Arab and Al Moosa (2025) identifies limitations in the reporting of indirect costs, infrastructure requirements and equity considerations in published healthcare AI economic evaluations.

Wu et al. (2025) also identify methodological heterogeneity and inconsistent reporting of AI-specific costs as challenges when interpreting the economic literature.

These findings support the need for a transparent assessment of missing information.

## 3. Categories of missing information

### 3.1 Technology acquisition and licensing

Relevant information includes software licensing, procurement arrangements, hardware and system access costs.

If these costs are excluded, the estimated cost of an AI-assisted pathway may be understated.

### 3.2 Infrastructure and integration

AI implementation may require IT infrastructure, data integration, interoperability work, cybersecurity arrangements and technical support.

Failure to include these requirements may underestimate the resources needed for adoption.

### 3.3 Staff training and time

Implementation may require training for screening technicians, graders, clinicians, administrators and technical personnel.

Staff time has an opportunity cost even when it does not result in an additional cash payment.

Excluding training and staff time may therefore understate implementation costs.

### 3.4 Maintenance and monitoring

AI systems require ongoing maintenance, performance monitoring, software updates and potentially repeat validation.

A short evaluation may not capture these continuing costs.

### 3.5 Diagnostic performance and clinical consequences

Sensitivity and specificity influence the expected numbers of false-negative and false-positive results.

However, diagnostic performance alone does not establish the costs or health consequences of those errors.

The economic implications depend on subsequent clinical assessment, referral, treatment, follow-up and patient outcomes.

### 3.6 Attendance and follow-up

Screening attendance and follow-up adherence affect the number of people who receive screening and the extent to which detected abnormalities lead to appropriate care.

An AI system cannot be assumed to improve health outcomes if patients do not attend screening or complete necessary follow-up.

### 3.7 Long-term patient outcomes

Short-term diagnostic accuracy does not establish long-term changes in visual impairment, quality of life or healthcare utilisation.

Long-term benefits should only be included when supported by appropriate evidence or clearly identified modelling assumptions.

## 4. Potential effects on economic conclusions

| Missing information | Potential consequence |
|---|---|
| Software licensing | Underestimation of AI pathway costs |
| Hardware and infrastructure | Underestimation of implementation expenditure |
| IT integration | Underestimation of organisational resources |
| Training | Underestimation of staff and implementation costs |
| Maintenance | Underestimation of recurring costs |
| Clinical oversight | Underestimation of human review requirements |
| False-positive consequences | Incomplete estimation of unnecessary assessment costs |
| False-negative consequences | Incomplete estimation of missed disease consequences |
| Attendance and follow-up | Uncertain estimates of pathway effectiveness |
| Long-term health outcomes | Uncertain estimates of patient benefit |
| Equity effects | Incomplete understanding of distributional consequences |

These are potential directions of bias, not quantified effects.

## 5. Implications for the NHS case study

The NHS case study must distinguish between costs that are directly reported in UK evidence and costs reported in international studies.

International cost estimates should not be transferred directly into an NHS model without considering differences in prices, healthcare organisation, technology procurement, staffing and clinical pathways.

The analysis should also distinguish:

- Initial implementation expenditure.
- Recurring operating expenditure.
- Costs that vary with the number of people screened.
- Costs that are relatively fixed over the modelled period.
- Costs incurred elsewhere in the healthcare pathway.
- Costs that are not captured by the selected economic perspective.

## 6. Treatment of missing values

The research will use the following rules:

1. Missing costs will not automatically be assigned a value of zero.
2. Unavailable clinical parameters will not be replaced with unsupported estimates.
3. Published values will retain their original source, year, units and context.
4. Derived values will include the calculation method.
5. Illustrative assumptions will be labelled explicitly and kept separate from empirical findings.
6. Where an essential input is unavailable, the relevant model result will be identified as not estimable.
7. Sensitivity analysis will only use ranges supported by evidence or explicitly described as illustrative.

## 7. Consequences for interpretation

Missing data can affect both the size and direction of an estimated economic difference.

For example, excluding AI maintenance costs could make an AI pathway appear less expensive than it would be in routine operation.

Conversely, excluding costs associated with unnecessary manual assessment in a comparator pathway could make the existing pathway appear less expensive than it actually is.

The direction of bias therefore depends on which pathway and which resources are incompletely measured.

## 8. Research limitations

The available literature does not necessarily report every resource required for an NHS implementation.

Published evaluations may use different perspectives, time horizons, comparators and assumptions. This limits direct comparison between studies.

The project cannot establish a complete NHS budget impact where reliable NHS-specific cost and activity inputs are unavailable.

## 9. Conclusion

Missing economic information is a substantive limitation in the evaluation of AI-assisted diabetic retinopathy screening.

A credible economic assessment must account for implementation, infrastructure, workforce, maintenance, clinical consequences and long-term outcomes where evidence permits.

Where information is unavailable, the research will report the uncertainty rather than imply that the omitted item has no economic consequence.

The central implication is that favourable diagnostic performance or a favourable published cost-effectiveness estimate is insufficient, by itself, to establish that AI-assisted screening will generate economic value in the NHS.
