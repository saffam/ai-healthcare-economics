# AI-Assisted Versus Autonomous Diabetic Retinopathy Screening: Clinical and Economic Comparison

## 1. Purpose

This document compares two approaches to using artificial intelligence in diabetic retinopathy screening:

1. AI-assisted screening, in which AI supports a human screening professional or clinician.
2. Autonomous AI screening, in which an appropriately authorised system produces a screening assessment without requiring routine human interpretation of every eligible image.

The purpose is to identify differences in clinical responsibilities, workflow, costs, implementation requirements and potential economic value.

The comparison is conceptual and evidence-informed. It does not assume that either approach is universally superior.

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 AI-assisted screening

In an AI-assisted pathway, an AI system analyses retinal images and provides information to a human professional.

The human professional may review the images, consider the AI output, resolve uncertain cases and determine whether further assessment or referral is required.

The degree of human involvement depends on the system design and clinical pathway.

### 2.2 Autonomous AI screening

In an autonomous pathway, an AI system independently produces a defined screening result within its authorised intended use.

Depending on the system and pathway, results may include a recommendation for routine screening, an ungradable result or a recommendation for further clinical assessment.

Autonomous assessment does not eliminate the need for clinical governance, quality assurance, monitoring, escalation procedures or appropriate patient follow-up.

### 2.3 Important distinction

Autonomous screening does not mean autonomous healthcare.

Clinical pathways still require arrangements for patients with abnormal or uncertain results, technical failures, ungradable images and conditions outside the system's intended use.

## 3. Comparison of the two approaches

| Dimension | AI-assisted screening | Autonomous AI screening |
|---|---|---|
| Image interpretation | AI supports human interpretation | AI produces a result within its authorised scope |
| Human involvement | Routine human review or oversight | Human review may be concentrated on exceptions and follow-up |
| Staff responsibilities | Review, interpretation and decision-making | Governance, exception handling, escalation and pathway management |
| Workflow | May retain existing human grading processes | May change the allocation of grading and review tasks |
| Training | Staff need to understand AI outputs and limitations | Staff need to understand system operation, escalation and governance |
| Technology requirements | AI system and integration with human review | AI system, integration and reliable autonomous operating arrangements |
| Quality assurance | Human and AI performance need monitoring | System performance and exception handling need monitoring |
| Potential efficiency | May reduce review time or support consistency | May reduce routine manual interpretation where clinically appropriate |
| Potential risks | Automation bias and overreliance on AI | Automation errors, inappropriate reliance and failures outside intended use |
| Accountability | Shared responsibilities must be defined | Responsibilities must be defined across provider, clinical and technology governance |

The table describes possible pathway differences. It does not establish that any particular system achieves these benefits.

## 4. Clinical performance considerations

The economic implications of both approaches depend partly on diagnostic performance.

Relevant measures include:

- Sensitivity.
- Specificity.
- Image gradability.
- False-positive results.
- False-negative results.
- Referral rates.
- Performance across different patient populations.
- Performance under routine service conditions.

Sensitivity and specificity must be interpreted in relation to the study population, disease definition, image acquisition method and reference standard.

Performance estimates from one AI system must not automatically be applied to another system.

## 5. Economic implications

### 5.1 AI-assisted screening

Potential costs include:

- Software licensing.
- IT infrastructure.
- System integration.
- Staff training.
- Human image review.
- Clinical oversight.
- Maintenance and monitoring.

Potential economic benefits may arise if AI reduces review time, improves workflow organisation or supports more consistent assessment.

However, time saved is not automatically equivalent to cash savings or increased productivity. The service must establish how the released staff capacity is used.

### 5.2 Autonomous screening

Potential costs include:

- Software licensing.
- Hardware and infrastructure.
- System integration.
- Validation and performance monitoring.
- Staff training.
- Exception handling.
- Clinical escalation.
- Maintenance and technical support.
- Regulatory and governance requirements.

Potential economic benefits may arise from reduced routine human grading requirements or changes in screening capacity.

However, autonomous screening may still require substantial human involvement for ungradable images, abnormal results, technical problems, quality assurance and clinical follow-up.

It must not be assumed that autonomous screening eliminates workforce costs.

## 6. Workforce implications

AI-assisted screening may change the nature of professional work by supporting image interpretation and prioritisation.

Autonomous screening may change the distribution of work more substantially, potentially shifting staff activity towards exception handling, quality assurance, patient communication and clinical escalation.

Both approaches may require new skills and responsibilities.

A proper economic evaluation should consider:

- Staff time before and after implementation.
- Training time.
- Changes in professional responsibilities.
- Additional technical and clinical support.
- Whether released capacity is redeployed.
- Whether staffing levels or expenditure actually change.

## 7. Human trust and accountability

Trust is relevant to both approaches.

In AI-assisted screening, professionals need to understand when to consider, question or override AI outputs.

In autonomous screening, trust depends on appropriate validation, transparency about system limitations, reliable monitoring and clear escalation arrangements.

The systematic review by Tun et al. (2025) identifies transparency, training, usability, reliability, credibility, ethics and professional autonomy as relevant themes in healthcare workers' trust in AI-based clinical decision support.

Trust should be appropriate to demonstrated system performance and limitations, rather than based on confidence in technology alone.

## 8. Institutional and implementation requirements

Both approaches depend on organisational readiness.

Important considerations include:

- Compatibility with existing screening systems.
- Data quality and interoperability.
- IT infrastructure.
- Procurement and licensing.
- Staff training.
- Clinical governance.
- Technical support.
- Performance monitoring.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Patient communication.
- Arrangements for failures and exceptions.

The implementation framework described by Powis et al. (2026) highlights the importance of organisational, technical, quality, safety and equity-related factors in healthcare AI implementation.

## 9. Implications for the NHS case study

The NHS diabetic eye screening pathway provides the institutional context for this research.

The existing pathway must be described accurately before the economic consequences of an AI-enabled alternative can be estimated.

The research should not assume that a model developed in another country or healthcare system can be transferred directly to the NHS.

A proposed autonomous pathway would need to be evaluated against the applicable clinical, regulatory, commissioning and service requirements.

The NICE Evidence Standards Framework provides a relevant structure for considering evidence requirements for digital health technologies.

## 10. Implications for the economic model

The economic model should keep the two approaches separate.

For AI-assisted screening, relevant parameters may include:

- Human review time.
- AI-related costs.
- Changes in review workload.
- Clinical oversight requirements.
- Referral and follow-up activity.

For autonomous screening, relevant parameters may include:

- The proportion of cases receiving an autonomous result.
- The proportion requiring human assessment.
- Ungradable image rates.
- Exception-handling costs.
- Clinical escalation costs.
- Ongoing system monitoring.

A numerical comparison should only be produced when sufficiently reliable and comparable inputs are available.

The model must not assume that autonomous screening has zero human review costs or that AI-assisted screening necessarily produces additional costs.

## 11. Limitations

The available evidence may differ in system design, clinical setting, disease definitions, study populations and evaluation methods.

Published evidence for autonomous screening cannot automatically be applied to AI-assisted systems, and vice versa.

The comparison in this document is primarily a structured analytical framework. It is not a direct head-to-head economic evaluation of two named technologies.

## 12. Conclusion

AI-assisted and autonomous diabetic retinopathy screening represent different approaches to organising clinical screening work.

AI-assisted systems retain routine human involvement in interpretation, whereas autonomous systems may produce defined screening assessments without routine human interpretation of every eligible image.

Their economic value depends on more than diagnostic accuracy. It also depends on implementation costs, staff requirements, clinical outcomes, exception handling, organisational readiness, patient follow-up and governance.

The appropriate approach for the NHS must therefore be evaluated using pathway-specific clinical and economic evidence rather than assumptions about automation.
