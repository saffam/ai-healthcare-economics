# Diagnostic Errors and Referral Consequences in AI-Assisted Diabetic Retinopathy Screening

## 1. Purpose

This document examines how false-positive and false-negative results may affect clinical pathways and economic outcomes.

## 2. Diagnostic classifications

A screening test can produce four principal outcomes:

- True positive: disease is identified in a person who has the condition.
- False positive: a person is incorrectly identified as having a potentially significant finding.
- True negative: a person without the condition receives a negative result.
- False negative: disease is not identified in a person who has the condition.

## 3. False-positive consequences

False-positive results may lead to:

- Additional clinical assessments.
- Additional imaging.
- Specialist referrals.
- Patient anxiety.
- Additional staff workload.
- Additional healthcare expenditure.

A false-positive result does not necessarily mean that a referral was inappropriate. Referral thresholds and the clinical pathway must be considered.

## 4. False-negative consequences

False-negative results may delay identification of disease.

Potential consequences include:

- Delayed clinical assessment.
- Delayed treatment.
- Progression of disease.
- Potential deterioration in visual outcomes.
- Additional future healthcare requirements.

The monetary consequences cannot be quantified without appropriate evidence on subsequent clinical outcomes and costs.

## 5. Sensitivity and specificity

Sensitivity measures the proportion of people with the target condition who receive a positive result.

Specificity measures the proportion of people without the target condition who receive a negative result.

Neither measure independently establishes the economic value of a screening technology.

## 6. Referral implications

Referral rates depend on diagnostic performance, disease prevalence, screening thresholds, image quality and clinical pathway design.

A higher referral rate does not automatically indicate better or worse performance.

It must be examined alongside the appropriateness of referrals, missed cases and downstream outcomes.

## 7. Implications for the NHS economic model

The model should distinguish:

- True-positive results.
- False-negative results.
- True-negative results.
- False-positive results.
- Ungradable images.
- Repeat imaging.
- Clinical assessment.
- Referral and follow-up.

Where these inputs are unavailable, the model should identify the limitation rather than generate unsupported estimates.

## 8. Limitations

Diagnostic accuracy estimates may vary across populations, equipment, image acquisition methods and reference standards.

Published accuracy estimates should not automatically be transferred between AI systems or healthcare settings.

## 9. Conclusion

Diagnostic errors have both clinical and economic implications.

A credible evaluation must consider the consequences of incorrect and uncertain screening results throughout the healthcare pathway.

The economic implications of diagnostic accuracy cannot be assessed independently of referral, follow-up and patient outcomes.
