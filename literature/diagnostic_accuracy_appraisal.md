# Clinical Evidence Appraisal: AI-Assisted Diabetic Retinopathy Screening

## 1. Purpose

This document assesses the relevance and limitations of selected diagnostic accuracy studies.

Diagnostic accuracy is an important component of the economic evaluation, but it is not equivalent to clinical effectiveness or cost-effectiveness.

## 2. Comparative systematic review

Tahir et al. (2025) compared AI and manual screening using 25 observational studies and a large image evidence base.

The abstract reports different pooled estimates for dilated and undilated eyes. AI showed higher pooled sensitivity in the reported comparisons, while manual screening showed higher specificity.

### Appraisal

The review is useful because it directly compares AI and manual screening.

However, the studies may differ in:

- Image acquisition.
- Dilation status.
- Disease definitions.
- Reference standards.
- AI systems.
- Screening populations.
- Study design.

The pooled values should not be treated as universal NHS performance parameters.

## 3. Prospective clinical validation

Ipp et al. (2021) evaluated an autonomous AI system in a prospective multicentre diagnostic study involving 942 people with diabetes.

The study reported sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 88% for more-than-mild diabetic retinopathy, and sensitivity of 97% and specificity of 90% for vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy.

### Appraisal

The prospective multicentre design provides useful clinical validation evidence.

However:

- The results concern a particular AI system.
- The study was not an NHS England implementation study.
- Diagnostic performance depends on the study protocol and population.
- The study does not by itself establish economic value.

## 4. Real-world diagnostic accuracy evidence

Uy et al. (2023) reviewed prospective diagnostic accuracy evidence for referable diabetic retinopathy and reported pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates.

A separate 2024 real-world systematic review also reported high pooled sensitivity and specificity among gradable images.

### Appraisal

Real-world evidence is relevant because performance outside controlled research settings may differ from pivotal validation studies.

However, results among gradable images may not represent the performance of the entire screening pathway. Image acquisition failures and ungradable images must also be considered.

## 5. Implications for economic modelling

The model must not use a single pooled estimate without considering:

- The intended disease threshold.
- Whether images are dilated.
- The target population.
- The reference standard.
- The proportion of images that are ungradable.
- Whether human review is retained.
- Whether the system is autonomous or decision-support based.

Sensitivity and specificity must be linked to a defined screening population and pathway.

## 6. Overall judgement

The evidence indicates that AI can achieve clinically useful diagnostic performance in diabetic retinopathy screening.

It does not establish that all systems perform equally well, that all settings will achieve the same results, or that AI screening will necessarily improve patient outcomes or reduce NHS expenditure.

The economic model must therefore treat diagnostic performance as context-dependent and examine uncertainty.
