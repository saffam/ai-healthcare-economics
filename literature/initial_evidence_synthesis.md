# Initial Evidence Synthesis: Economics and Implementation of Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare

**Project:** The Economics of Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare

**Evidence review status:** Preliminary evidence synthesis

**Evidence search date:** 22 November 2026

## 1. Purpose

This document presents an initial synthesis of selected academic and institutional sources relevant to the economic, organisational and human implications of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare.

The purpose is to establish an initial evidence base for the wider literature review. The selected sources address economic evaluation, professional trust, institutional implementation, ethical governance and health technology assessment.

This synthesis is preliminary. It does not represent the completed results of a systematic review, and the findings should be interpreted in light of the methods and limitations of each source.

## 2. Economic Evaluation of Healthcare AI

Wu et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review of economic evaluations of AI-assisted healthcare technologies. The review included 52 studies from 15 countries, covering applications including diabetic retinopathy screening, cancer detection and cardiovascular disease.

The authors reported that cost-utility analysis was the predominant economic evaluation method. They also found that most included studies reported favourable economic conclusions concerning AI-based interventions.

However, the review identified important methodological limitations. Reporting of AI-specific costs was inconsistent, and the included studies differed in their analytical approaches. The authors also identified potential publication bias as a limitation affecting the interpretation of the available evidence.

These findings indicate that favourable economic conclusions in published studies should not automatically be interpreted as proof that healthcare AI is universally cost-effective or cost-saving.

For the present research, an important implication is the need to examine the completeness of cost reporting, the choice of comparator, the time horizon and the healthcare setting in which each economic evaluation was conducted.

## 3. Trust and Human Decision-Making

Tun et al. (2025) examined factors influencing healthcare workers' trust in AI-based clinical decision support systems through a systematic review of 27 studies.

The review identified several themes associated with trust, including transparency, training, usability, clinical reliability, validation, ethical considerations, human-centred design and professional autonomy.

The findings suggest that trust should not be treated simply as a willingness to adopt technology. It is connected to the perceived reliability and suitability of a system, the user's understanding of its limitations and the extent to which professional judgement remains meaningful.

For the present research, this supports an examination of appropriate trust rather than an assumption that greater adoption or reliance necessarily represents better decision-making.

The relationship between trust and economic value also requires further investigation. Training, system usability, review requirements and workflow integration may influence both implementation costs and the extent to which potential benefits are realised.

## 4. Institutional Implementation

Powis et al. (2026) developed a framework for research into the implementation of AI in healthcare settings. Their synthesis identified 12 domains and 63 constructs across themes including design and development, organisational resources and readiness, deployment and maintenance, quality and safety, and equity.

The framework highlights the importance of organisational resources, workforce readiness, institutional acceptance, technical integration and ongoing maintenance.

These findings are relevant to the question of why the same AI application may produce different results across healthcare organisations.

They also reinforce the need to distinguish between the technical capability of an AI system and the ability of an organisation to implement, maintain and evaluate it effectively.

The framework provides a conceptual basis for examining institutional variation, although it is not itself a direct economic evaluation of a particular AI intervention.

## 5. Governance and Human Autonomy

The World Health Organization (2021) published guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health. The guidance considers the opportunities and risks associated with AI and establishes principles intended to support responsible design, deployment and use.

Its emphasis on human autonomy, safety, transparency, accountability and public benefit is relevant to the present research.

The guidance supports an approach in which AI is assessed not only through technical and economic performance but also through its implications for individuals, healthcare professionals and institutions.

For this project, governance will be considered part of the conditions necessary for sustainable implementation rather than as an issue separate from economic evaluation.

## 6. Evidence Standards and Economic Evaluation

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) provides an Evidence Standards Framework for Digital Health Technologies.

The framework includes expectations concerning evidence of effectiveness, the description of existing care pathways, budget impact and economic evaluation where appropriate.

Its guidance identifies relevant implementation costs, including technology costs, staffing, training, infrastructure, updating and maintenance.

This is important because the economic assessment of an AI intervention should consider the costs of the pathway in which it is implemented rather than only the purchase price of the technology.

The framework also distinguishes budget impact from cost-effectiveness. These concepts answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 7. Preliminary Cross-Source Synthesis

The initial sources indicate five important themes.

First, published economic evaluations frequently report favourable results, but methodological variation and incomplete cost reporting limit the comparability of those findings.

Second, trust in AI-assisted healthcare decisions is influenced by system reliability, transparency, training, usability and the preservation of appropriate professional autonomy.

Third, organisational resources, workforce readiness and integration into existing workflows may influence whether potential technological benefits are realised.

Fourth, governance, accountability and human autonomy are relevant to the conditions under which healthcare AI can be responsibly implemented.

Fifth, economic evaluation requires consideration of the full implementation pathway, including staffing, training, infrastructure and ongoing costs.

Taken together, these themes support an integrated approach to healthcare AI evaluation that combines economic, institutional, human and policy considerations.

## 8. Limitations of the Initial Evidence

This document is based on an initial selection of sources and should not be interpreted as a comprehensive account of the literature.

Some study-level information has been extracted from publication abstracts and publicly available summaries. Full-text examination and detailed methodological appraisal remain necessary.

The sources also differ in purpose and design. Economic systematic reviews, trust reviews, implementation frameworks and institutional guidance cannot be treated as equivalent forms of evidence.

The international findings may not be directly transferable to NHS England without considering differences in healthcare financing, institutional arrangements and clinical practice.
