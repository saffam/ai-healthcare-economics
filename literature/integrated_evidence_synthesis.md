# Integrated Evidence Synthesis

## The Economics of Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare

**Project status:** Independent secondary research  
**Geographical focus:** NHS England, with international comparative evidence  
**Evidence position:** Preliminary synthesis; source verification remains necessary before final publication

---

## 1. Purpose

This document integrates the project's literature and analytical frameworks concerning the economic, clinical, organisational, workforce, trust and policy implications of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare.

The purpose is not to establish that AI is inherently beneficial or harmful. It is to examine the conditions under which healthcare AI may generate meaningful value, the costs and risks that accompany implementation, and the institutional arrangements required for responsible adoption.

The synthesis draws on published research, policy guidance and the project's preliminary evidence registers. It is a structured interpretation of the available material, not a new clinical trial, systematic review with completed formal screening, or empirical evaluation of an NHS AI deployment.

## 2. Economic value is conditional, not automatic

Published economic evaluations suggest that some healthcare AI applications may be cost-effective, cost-saving or economically beneficial. However, favourable results in individual studies should not be interpreted as proof that AI adoption will generate savings across healthcare systems.

The systematic review by Wu and colleagues examines economic evaluations of AI-assisted healthcare technologies and identifies a substantial body of published economic evidence. The review also highlights methodological differences and limitations in how AI-specific costs are reported.

The systematic review by El Arab and Al Moosa similarly examines cost-effectiveness and budget-impact evidence. Its discussion of indirect costs, infrastructure and equity is relevant to the possibility that economic evaluations may omit important components of implementation.

These findings support a central distinction:

**Technical performance is not equivalent to economic value.**

An AI system may demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy without reducing total healthcare expenditure. It may improve efficiency in one part of a pathway while increasing expenditure elsewhere. It may also require substantial investment before benefits become observable.

An economic evaluation should therefore consider the complete care pathway rather than the purchase price or technical performance of the software alone.

## 3. The importance of implementation costs

The financial consequences of healthcare AI extend beyond initial procurement.

Relevant cost categories include:

- Software acquisition, licensing and updates.
- Hardware, infrastructure and integration with existing systems.
- Data preparation, quality assurance and information governance.
- Staff training and changes to professional workflows.
- Technical support, maintenance and monitoring.
- Clinical oversight and review of uncertain outputs.
- Costs arising from false-positive and false-negative results.
- Transition costs associated with changing existing services.
- Opportunity costs when financial or staff resources are redirected.

The NICE Evidence Standards Framework provides a relevant policy framework for considering evidence requirements for digital health technologies. Its associated guidance identifies cost categories that may be relevant to implementation and economic assessment.

A credible evaluation should distinguish the initial implementation budget from recurring costs and should avoid treating unmeasured costs as zero.

## 4. Diagnostic performance and patient outcomes

Diagnostic accuracy is an important component of healthcare AI evaluation, but it is not a complete measure of clinical benefit.

Sensitivity and specificity describe different aspects of test performance. Their implications depend on the prevalence of the condition, the population being tested, the reference standard and the consequences of diagnostic errors.

False-positive results may generate additional appointments, investigations and referrals. False-negative results may delay further assessment and potentially affect patient outcomes.

The diabetic retinopathy case study provides a useful context for examining these issues. Published research has evaluated AI-supported and autonomous approaches to retinal screening, including their diagnostic performance and potential economic implications.

However, results from different studies cannot automatically be treated as interchangeable. Differences in populations, imaging procedures, disease definitions, clinical pathways and reference standards may limit direct comparison.

The project therefore treats diagnostic performance estimates as evidence requiring source-level verification before they are used in any economic calculation.

## 5. Attendance and pathway effects

The economic value of screening technologies may depend on whether patients complete the screening and referral pathway.

A technically accurate screening system cannot generate its intended population-level benefit if patients do not attend, images cannot be graded, referrals are not completed or follow-up arrangements are ineffective.

Attendance, repeat testing, referral completion and treatment access should therefore be considered alongside diagnostic performance.

The NHS diabetic eye screening pathway provides the institutional context for this analysis. Published screening coverage statistics describe service activity and participation, but they do not establish that AI caused changes in attendance, coverage or patient outcomes.

The project will not interpret general changes in screening coverage as evidence of an AI intervention effect.

## 6. Workforce productivity and professional judgement

AI may change the distribution of tasks between clinical professionals, administrative staff and technical systems.

Potential benefits include reduced repetitive work, support for prioritisation and more efficient processing of information. Potential costs include training requirements, additional verification work, technical troubleshooting and the need to review uncertain or incorrect outputs.

Time saved should not automatically be described as a financial saving or productivity improvement.

A productivity claim requires evidence that released time is used productively, that service capacity changes or that resources can be redeployed. A reduction in one task may simply transfer work to another professional or department.

The distinction between AI-assisted and autonomous systems is particularly important. In AI-assisted screening, a professional remains involved in the decision process. In autonomous screening, the system may produce a defined result without routine human interpretation at that stage.

These arrangements have different implications for accountability, staffing, workflow and professional autonomy.

## 7. Trust and appropriate reliance

Trust in healthcare AI is influenced by more than technical accuracy.

The systematic review by Tun and colleagues identifies factors relevant to healthcare workers' trust in AI-based clinical decision-support systems, including transparency, usability, familiarity, reliability, validation and professional autonomy.

These findings suggest that appropriate trust requires more than encouraging professionals to use a technology. Healthcare workers need sufficient information and training to understand the system's purpose, limitations and intended role.

Trust should not mean unquestioning acceptance. Equally, a system should not be rejected solely because it is unfamiliar.

The relevant objective is calibrated reliance: using AI when its intended application and evidence justify reliance, while recognising circumstances in which human review, escalation or an alternative process is necessary.

Patient trust also requires attention to communication, transparency, consent where applicable, privacy and the availability of meaningful human oversight.

## 8. Institutional differences

The economic and clinical effects of a technology may differ between healthcare organisations.

Relevant differences include:

- Existing digital infrastructure.
- Workforce availability and training.
- Patient population characteristics.
- Service volumes and referral patterns.
- Local screening and treatment pathways.
- Procurement and licensing arrangements.
- Technical support and maintenance capacity.
- Governance and accountability arrangements.
- Organisational readiness and implementation experience.

An intervention that is economically attractive in one setting may not produce the same results in another.

International evidence can inform the analysis, but its transferability to NHS England must be considered rather than assumed.

The project will therefore distinguish evidence generated in the NHS from evidence generated in other health systems.

## 9. Regulation, reimbursement and governance

Responsible implementation requires governance arrangements that address safety, effectiveness, accountability, transparency and equity.

The World Health Organization's guidance on ethics and governance of AI for health provides an international framework for considering human rights, accountability and the ethical implications of healthcare AI.

The NICE Evidence Standards Framework provides a relevant UK framework for evidence expectations concerning digital health technologies.

These frameworks should not be treated as interchangeable. They have different purposes and institutional roles.

The project examines how regulation, evidence requirements, procurement, reimbursement and organisational governance may influence adoption and long-term sustainability.

It does not provide legal advice or certify that any particular technology meets regulatory requirements.

## 10. Equity and distributional effects

Aggregate economic benefits may conceal differences between population groups.

AI systems may perform differently across populations or settings, and access to AI-supported services may depend on geography, digital infrastructure, service capacity and other institutional factors.

An evaluation should consider who receives the benefits, who bears the costs and who may be exposed to additional risks.

Relevant questions include:

- Are diagnostic performance differences reported across relevant population groups?
- Are some patients more likely to experience ungradable results or additional referrals?
- Does implementation improve access for underserved populations?
- Could digital infrastructure or service availability create unequal access?
- Are the costs and benefits distributed fairly?

Where the evidence does not permit a reliable distributional assessment, this limitation should be stated explicitly.

## 11. Evidence quality and limitations

The available evidence is heterogeneous.

Studies may differ in design, population, technology, clinical setting, comparator, time horizon, outcome measures and economic perspective.

Additional limitations include:

- Reliance on modelled rather than observed long-term outcomes.
- Incomplete reporting of implementation and maintenance costs.
- Limited evidence from routine clinical deployment.
- Potential conflicts of interest.
- Differences in diagnostic reference standards.
- Limited reporting of distributional effects.
- Uncertainty about whether results transfer between health systems.

Abstract-level information is insufficient for final verification of detailed study methods or economic inputs.

The project will therefore retain a distinction between preliminary evidence extraction and verified full-text evidence.

No numerical result should be used as a definitive model input until its source, definition, population, units and methodological context have been checked.

## 12. Integrated interpretation

Taken together, the evidence supports six broad interpretations.

1. Healthcare AI may generate economic value, but benefits depend on the application, implementation context and full costs.
2. Diagnostic accuracy is important but does not independently establish improved patient outcomes or cost-effectiveness.
3. Workforce effects depend on task redistribution, workflow design, professional oversight and the use of time released.
4. Appropriate trust requires reliable evidence, transparency, training and meaningful human accountability.
5. Institutional differences may substantially affect implementation costs and realised benefits.
6. Governance and equity considerations are integral to economic sustainability, rather than optional additions.

These interpretations are analytical conclusions from the literature and frameworks considered. They are not claims that a particular NHS AI system has already generated a measured economic benefit.

## 13. Implications for the diabetic retinopathy case study

The diabetic retinopathy screening case study will examine AI-supported screening as an application through which these questions can be explored.

The analysis should distinguish:

- AI-assisted screening from autonomous screening.
- Diagnostic performance from clinical outcomes.
- Screening activity from completed patient pathways.
- Time savings from measurable productivity.
- Direct expenditure from full implementation costs.
- Published observed results from modelled projections.
- NHS evidence from international evidence.
- Verified inputs from illustrative assumptions.

Where sufficiently reliable NHS-specific inputs are unavailable, the project will report the data gap and its implications rather than manufacture a cost-effectiveness estimate.

## 14. Overall conclusion

The economic case for healthcare AI cannot be determined by technical performance alone.

A defensible assessment requires consideration of the full care pathway, implementation and maintenance costs, diagnostic consequences, workforce effects, patient outcomes, professional trust, institutional readiness, governance and equity.

The evidence reviewed provides reasons to investigate the potential value of healthcare AI, but it also demonstrates why economic claims must be context-specific and supported by transparent methods.

The central conclusion of this independent research project is therefore conditional:

**Healthcare AI can contribute to economic and clinical value when its performance, implementation costs, organisational context, human oversight and distributional consequences are evaluated together.**

The strength of any specific conclusion will depend on the quality and completeness of the underlying evidence.
