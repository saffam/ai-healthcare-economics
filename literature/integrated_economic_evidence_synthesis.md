# Integrated Economic Evidence Synthesis

## 1. Purpose

This document synthesises the core economic evidence identified for the independent research project. It focuses on the relationship between AI diagnostic performance, implementation costs, clinical pathways and economic value.

## 2. General healthcare AI economic evaluations

Wu et al. (2025) and El Arab and Al Moosa (2025) provide systematic-review evidence on economic evaluations of AI in healthcare.

The existence of published studies reporting favourable economic outcomes does not establish that healthcare AI is generally cost-effective. Economic evaluations differ in technologies, clinical settings, populations, comparators, time horizons, outcome measures and cost assumptions.

The economic evidence must therefore be interpreted at the level of the specific intervention and decision context.

## 3. Diabetic retinopathy screening

Wang et al. (2024) modelled AI diabetic-retinopathy screening in a large Chinese screening programme. Their analysis illustrates that the diagnostic configuration with the highest accuracy need not be the configuration with the most favourable economic result.

This finding is important because sensitivity and specificity have different economic consequences. Increasing sensitivity can identify additional people requiring follow-up, while changes in specificity can alter the number of false-positive referrals. The economic implications depend on prevalence, pathway capacity, follow-up, treatment and costs.

The model's numerical results must not be transferred directly to NHS England.

Leigh et al. (2026) reviewed 18 economic analyses of AI versus manual grading for diabetic retinopathy. The review highlights methodological variation and the importance of context-specific parameters, follow-up adherence and the trade-off between diagnostic performance and cost-effectiveness.

## 4. Cost categories

A complete economic assessment should consider:

- Software and technology acquisition.
- Hardware and infrastructure.
- Integration with existing systems.
- Staff training.
- Implementation and workflow redesign.
- Maintenance and updates.
- Monitoring, audit and quality assurance.
- Human review and exception handling.
- Follow-up and referral.
- Costs associated with diagnostic errors.
- Opportunity costs and displaced activity.

A cost category should not be treated as zero merely because a study does not report it.

## 5. Productivity and workforce

Time saved by automation is not automatically equivalent to cash-releasing savings or increased productivity.

A productivity claim requires evidence that saved time is converted into additional useful activity, reduced resource use, improved access or another measurable output. Staff time may instead be redeployed, absorbed by additional tasks or offset by oversight and exception handling.

## 6. Transferability to NHS England

International evidence can inform model structure and identify relevant variables. Numerical values require a separate transferability assessment.

Differences in screening coverage, disease prevalence, referral thresholds, staff roles, technology procurement, follow-up adherence and service costs may change economic results.

## 7. Evidence limitations

The currently verified bibliography is not a completed full-text extraction. Abstract-level findings should not be used as final model parameters.

The current economic input file has 16 unresolved parameters. Accordingly, no NHS-specific incremental cost-effectiveness result is established.

## 8. Conclusion

The available literature supports the need for context-specific economic evaluation of healthcare AI. It does not support a general conclusion that AI screening is necessarily cost-saving or cost-effective.

For the diabetic-retinopathy case study, the next requirement is a transparent NHS pathway model populated only with appropriately sourced, verified and applicable inputs.
